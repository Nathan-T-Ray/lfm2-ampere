## 1. The wall is structural: the weight stream is chained to the dependency chain

### RESULT OF THE KILL-TEST: the persistent design is refuted; a graph-side prefetch works

`tests/bench_runahead.cu` (`make bench-runahead`), three runs on GPU 0 with both GPUs otherwise
idle. It uses `bench_mkbar`'s stage, its 504-block grid and its V2/V7 barriers, with counters reset
outside the timing. Every variant passed a `memcmp` gate against `k_gemv_q4_0` before it was timed
(29/29 configs in run 3, in all three shapes). `bench_mkbar` was re-run first and reproduced §13.14
(boundary 1.176 µs, V2 barrier 2.537 µs).

Per-stage overhead above the persistent no-barrier floor, in µs. Run 3, median of 7 round-robin
rounds:

| shape | CUDA graph | V2 barrier, published shape | best *correct* persistent run-ahead | graph + tail L2 prefetch, best |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| (B) 12288 rows × 31 stages | +0.83 | +2.00 | +1.74 | **−0.01** (1024 rows) |
| (C) 2048 rows × 130 stages | +1.51 | +2.73 | +2.16 | **+0.88** (768 rows) |
| (D) engine-like layer: 6144/64/2048/64/64/64/12288/64/8192 rows, × 12 | +1.07 | +2.27 | +2.08 | **+0.85** (1024 rows) |

**The prediction failed.** I predicted ≤ 0.5 µs and set the falsifier at "above the launch
boundary". The best correct run-ahead variant costs +1.74 µs at the published shape, against
+0.83 µs for a graph. A persistent kernel loses to the graph in all three shapes. The persistent
design below and its 520–580 tok/s estimate are **withdrawn**.

What I tried to rescue it:
- run-ahead issued before the arrival (run 1): no better, and the shared-memory version was worse;
- a split barrier that fences, arrives, issues the run-ahead and then waits on an acquire load;
- L2 versus shared-memory run-ahead, at three sizes;
- the honest variant, which re-reads the activations through L2 every stage, as a real persistent
  kernel must;
- a fence-free barrier, as a diagnostic only.

**Mechanism (inferred, not measured directly).** In the small shapes the fences account for
0.9–1.1 µs of the barrier. Even with no fences at all, the barrier only matches a launch boundary.
Run-ahead in flight during the barrier also does not shorten the stage. At 2048 rows the *entire*
next stage was in flight during a fence-free barrier, and the stage time did not move: 4.112 against
4.116 µs.

The barrier's own signals (atomics and polls) travel through the same L2 and DRAM queues as the
bulk data, so they wait behind it. A launch boundary's signalling does not, which is why the
graph-side prefetch overlaps and the barrier-side one does not. A dedicated producer-warp ring
would wait in the same queue, so I would not build it.

**What does work: a tail prefetch.**
- **What it does:** after finishing their own row, the last warps of each GEMV issue
  `prefetch.global.L2::evict_last` for the first ~1 MB of the next big GEMV, i.e. 768–1024 rows of
  1152 B. The best size is about bandwidth × the launch gap.
- **Timing and size are critical:**
  - issued *before* the warp's own row, it gains nothing or loses a little, because it competes
    with the current kernel's loads;
  - larger sizes (2k–8k rows) are worse;
  - spreading the same bytes over fewer, later warps did not help.
- **At the published shape** it makes the graph as fast as a barrier-free persistent kernel.
- **In the engine-like layer** it gives 48.35 against 50.39 µs per layer (−4.0%), or −5.0% against
  the same kernel without the prefetch.
- **No change to the arithmetic**, so `tools/ab.py` should stay 7/7.

**Revised estimate for the engine: ~+4–5%, i.e. ~460–465 tok/s.** This is still an estimate: the
engine's kernels have other launch shapes, and gate_up's prefetch target depends on the data,
although it is known by the time gate_up runs. The tail prefetch replaces the streamer-branch design
below. It needs no extra graph branch, no progress counter and no done flag.

Side findings:
- Replaying the same launches as a real CUDA graph is 0.62 µs per stage faster than the stream
  launches that `bench_mkbar` times under the label "separate launches (graph)" (16.40 against
  17.03 µs at 12288 rows).
- Staging the 2.3 KB activation vector in shared memory once per block, instead of per-warp L1
  reads, made the barrier-free 12288-row GEMV 2.4% faster. It cost more in the small shapes.

### Why every attempt so far has hit the same wall

In every kernel in the engine, a block starts loading weights only **after** its dependency has
resolved, whether that is a launch boundary (graph) or a barrier (`megakernel.cuh`, `mkbar.cuh`).
But the weights do not depend on the activations. The one exception is expert addresses, which
depend on top-4. `grep` finds no `cp.async`, no ring buffer and no run-ahead anywhere in `src/` or
`tests/`.

On sm_86 the only storage that survives a kernel boundary is L2 (6 MB). Ampere has no programmatic
dependent launch; that arrived on sm_90. So a graph of kernels can never let the weight stream run
ahead of the compute chain by more than L2 allows, and in practice it doesn't run ahead at all.
Each node pays the toll, the ramp and the first-byte latency in series.

That is also what the megakernel campaign measured. §13.14's own diagnosis of the 2.5 µs barrier
is *"the rendezvous itself: waiting for the last block, releasing to 504 pollers, and restarting
with no overlap."* All 14 barrier variants, both stage-size regimes and the cadence sweep had the
same structure: barrier, then `mk_stage_gemv_q4_0` issues its first weight load. **Run-ahead was
never measured.** What §13.13/§13.14 closed is "a persistent kernel with no overlap". The closed
result does not cover a persistent kernel whose memory stream ignores the barrier.

The same is true of the three "fusing into `moe_gate_up` loses" results (FUSE_ROUTER, T4r, T2) and
of law (g). Each placed dependent work (a selection, a reduction, a `__syncthreads`) in front of
the weight loads.

Phase 4z.6, the L2 prefetch, is not a refutation either. It was:
1. issued from a kernel **on** the critical path (`k_swiglu`);
2. confined to a ~1.5 µs window;
3. aimed at **data-dependent** targets (expert heads, knowable only after top-4);
4. at default L2 priority, while 21 MB of expert data streamed through a 6 MB L2.

None of those four has to be true.

### ~~The design I would build: a persistent, weight-streaming kernel~~ (REFUTED, see the result above)

*Kept for the record. The kill-test below falsified it.*

- One block per SM (84 on the Ti, 82 on the 3090; read `multiProcessorCount`, don't hardcode it).
- **Producer warp(s)** walk a host-built, per-block list of weight tiles in consumption order and
  issue `cp.async` (via `cuda::memcpy_async` + `cuda::barrier`; compiled fine for sm_86 here) into
  a shared-memory ring. They stall only when the ring is full, or on an expert tile whose layer's
  `d_idx` isn't published yet. **They never wait on activations.**
- **Consumer warps** wait on the stage's activation-ready counter (acquire), read the Q8_1 vector
  once into shared memory, compute their rows from the ring with the *unchanged* per-row lane loop
  and `warp_sum` (your §13.6 rule, so bit-identical), then publish (release).
- Use two rings: a **static ring** (dense weights, can run ahead across layers) and an **expert
  ring**. The static ring fills the router gap with the next layer's `in_proj` head.
- Persistent across tokens too. Layer 0's `in_proj` streams during `lm_head` and argmax, so the
  token boundary also disappears.

### Arithmetic that says it closes

Per ShortConv+MoE layer (28 of the 40):
- static: `in` 7.08 + `conv` 0.02 + `out` 2.36 + router 0.52 = 9.98 MB
- experts: 4 × 5.31 = 21.23 MB
- total: 31.2 MB = **33.1 µs of wire**

The chain, with the prologue fusions in §5, is in_proj → out_proj (+conv) → router → gate_up
(+top-4) → down (+swiglu): **5 handoffs**. At the 2.5 µs §13.14 measured, that is 12.5 µs, well
under 33 µs. So the layer is stream-bound, provided the ring covers the gaps. *(This is where the
argument failed. It assumed the handoffs overlap the stream. Measured, they don't: the barrier
waits behind the in-flight data.)*

| gap to cover | bytes at 941.8 GB/s | per SM |
|---|---|---|
| one handoff, 2.5 µs | 2.35 MB | 28 KB |
| router gap, ~5–6 µs (nothing static left in this layer) | 4.7–5.7 MB | 56–67 KB |

sm_86 allows up to 100 KB of shared memory per SM, so it fits. With weights arriving through the
ring, the router stage no longer needs the deep load unroll that gives `k_router_dot` its 127
registers, which was §13.12's residency problem.

**Estimate:** if this works, the step lands at wire plus a small exposed tail. Your own persistent
probe streamed at 878 GB/s (§13.12), which gives a ceiling of 1466.75 / 878 = 1.67 ms = **~600
tok/s**. A realistic target is **~520–580 tok/s**. That is an estimate, not a measurement.

### A graph-compatible version of the same idea (bit-identical, a few days) — SUPERSEDED

*Superseded by the tail prefetch in the result above, which is simpler and has been measured. The
estimate below was not.*

Add a **second branch to the CUDA graph**: one small "streamer" kernel (a few hundred threads)
that runs alongside the whole 343-node chain. It issues
`prefetch.global.L2::evict_last` (compiled fine for sm_86 here) for the **static** weights of the
next few stages, at most ~3–4 MB ahead of a progress counter. Each static kernel's block 0 bumps
that counter. The streamer exits on a done flag set by `k_argmax_final`.

The expert loads are already `__ldcs` (evict-first), so prefetched static lines should survive the
expert stream. The streamer also runs **during launch gaps**: it is not a node in the chain, so the
0.268 ms "unfillable" toll windows become fillable. That is why this is **not** bounded by the
488.9 ceiling, which assumed nothing could run in the gaps. The arithmetic is unchanged, so
`tools/ab.py` should stay 7/7.

This GPU also exposes a 4.125 MB persisting-L2 set-aside (`cudaDevAttrMaxPersistingL2CacheSize`
= 4325376, queried here) if the eviction hint alone isn't sticky enough.

**Estimate:** recovers 0.1–0.3 ms → **~460–510 tok/s**. It's cheap and tests the thesis
on the real engine before the rewrite.

### 3090 vs 3090 Ti

The design matters *more* on the plain 3090. It has 82 SMs, 936 GB/s and a lower boost clock, so
the latency-bound share of the step is larger. Size everything from device properties.
`tests/bandwidth_probe.cu` hardcodes 84.

## 2. The byte model charges `lm_head` once per position. A multi-token pass reads it once.

`tools/lkbytes.py`: `pass_mb_spec = lm_head_evals_spec(K) * LM_HEAD_MB + …` and
`pass_mb_batch = B * LM_HEAD_MB + …`. A pass over M vectors reads the 215.04 MB of Q6_K
**once** and does M dot products per row, exactly as the model already assumes for the dense
weights (`FIXED_MB`, charged once) and the experts (charged by the union).

Compute is no obstacle. At M=8 the dp4a work is roughly 37M warp-instructions, about 50 µs,
against ~228 µs of wire. It's less still with tensor cores (§3).

**REBRIEF A4, "find the hole":** the K−1 argument is wrong on its own terms. The logits "in hand"
are the ones at the position *before* the seed, which produced the seed. The seed itself has never
been through the model. Your `E = 1 + trigger·p1` counts the bonus token, which needs logits at the
last position too. So K positions need K logit vectors, and the legacy K count was right. It
doesn't matter once `lm_head` is read once per pass, but the "correction" should be reverted rather
than carried forward.

## 3. Multi-token kernels can be tensor-core AND bit-identical

> **UPDATE 2026-10-22: BUILT, as batched prefill** (src/prefill.cu; README "Batched prefill"). The int8 mma.m16n8k32 path consumes Q4_0 nibbles directly as A fragments, as proposed. Prefill is now 1.5-1.9x llama.cpp tuned at every length to 131k (13.9 s vs 24.2 s at 131k). The per-block terms are the decode path's own; the block ORDER is not, and bit-identity to the sequential path turned out to be the wrong target -- Q8_1 quantisation amplifies any 1e-7 reassociation into flipped int8 roundings, so the gate is the whole-corpus NLL (4.3493 vs llama.cpp 4.3503) plus byte-identity of every per-token kernel. Second round the same day: int8 attention scores with an exact fp16 window (0.26% attention error, under the 0.63% Q8_1 floor) and a 2-op GEMM epilogue took prefill to ~2x llama.cpp tuned from 4k to 131k (11.9 s vs 24.2 s at 131k; NLL 4.3502). The producer/consumer GEMM was built and measured 8-25% SLOWER on sm_86 (README, Second round).

The reason given for keeping M-pass verification on the exact M=1 path is bit-identity (§13.6).
Integer `mma.sync.m16n8k32` (u8×s8→s32 or s8×s8→s32, available on sm_86) computes a whole Q4_0 ×
Q8_1 block, 32 elements, in one k-step. Integer sums are exact, so each block's `sumi` is identical
to the dp4a result. Bit-identity then reduces to reproducing the **fp32 combine order**: per block,
`d4*(d8*sumi − 8*SUM)`; per lane, blocks `j = lane, lane+32, …` in order; then the same `warp_sum`
butterfly. That is a small, deterministic epilogue.

One kernel family then serves lossless verification, batching, and **batched prefill**. Prefill is
the largest user-visible gap: 526 s against llama.cpp's 34.5 s at 131k (15×), because prefill is
one decode step per prompt token.

## 4. A bit-identical, barrier-free norm fold does exist (ARCHITECTURE §2.3 challenge)

The claim "no barrier-free norm fusion can reproduce `(x*inv)*w` bit-for-bit, because the producer
cannot know `inv`" is true of **producer-side** folding only.

**Consumer-side recompute:** every consumer block reads the raw fp32 residual (8 KB) and the norm
weights (8 KB) from L2. Threads 0–255 then run `k_rmsnorm_q8_1`'s n == 2048 path verbatim: the
same 8 strided terms, the same 128/64/32 shared-memory tree, the same warp-0 butterfly, the same
`rsqrtf`, `(x*inv)*w` and `quantize_q8_1_warp2`. The Q8_1 vector goes into shared memory instead
of global. That gives the same inputs and the same instruction sequence, so the **pre-4y bytes,
bit for bit.** There is no cross-block communication; the producer→consumer kernel boundary
already exists.

It also removes §13.8's subnormal-`d` issue (14% of blocks). That issue comes from quantising the
*unnormalised* `x*w`; normalised activations sit around rms 1.

Two costs, and how to handle them:
1. **L2 traffic.** It is 16 KB per consumer block, which is fine for 64-block GEMVs and too much
   for `moe_gate_up`'s 1536 blocks (24.6 MB per layer). Grid-stride it to 168–252 blocks; your
   §13.13/§13.14 tests show the grid-stride port is bit-identical and shape is worth only 1–2%.
2. **The prologue's latency.** Hide it by **issuing the block's weight loads first** (the addresses
   don't depend on the norm), then running the prologue while they are in flight.

Expect roughly speed-neutral. The value is getting `ab.py` back as a gate across the 80 fold sites
and returning C1 to 191/825.

## 5. Smaller items (most are bit-identical)

1. **Host loop bubble (cheap, do first).** `gen` runs `run(); cudaStreamSynchronize(); take()`
   per token, a blocking 4-byte D2H, **including in `LK_BENCH`**. Prefill does a blocking H2D per
   token. The decode state is already device-resident (`d_token`, `d_pos`), so:
   - write each argmax into a device token ring at `d_pos`;
   - enqueue step N+1 before reading token N (EOS one step late, discard the extra);
   - in bench mode, launch N graphs back-to-back;
   - for prefill, upload the prompt once and have `k_embed_q6_K` read `prompt[d_pos]`.

   **Estimate:** 10–25 µs of GPU idle per token, about +0.5–1%. Measure it by comparing
   back-to-back replays against the current loop. That bubble is also inside the 2.266 ms the whole
   decomposition is fitted to.
2. **"Weights first" prologue fusions**, which counter law (g) whenever the address doesn't depend
   on the prologue:
   - `moe_swiglu` → `k_moe_down_accum` prologue (38 nodes; `d_idx` is already known). Issue the
     expert row loads (registers, or `cp.async` into shared memory to avoid T1's register lesson),
     then compute `silu(gate)*up` + Q8_1 for the 6144 values into shared memory.
   - `dense_swiglu` → `dense_down` (2 nodes).
   - `shortconv_conv` → the band-local `in_proj` epilogue you already identified, or the
     `out_proj` prologue with only block 0 writing `hist` (30 nodes).

   The upper bound from your own ablations is ~1% (swiglu) + ~1.1% (conv) + 68 × 0.78 µs of toll
   (2.3%), so **~2–4%**.
3. **Make bit-identity a property of the source, not of the compiler (REBRIEF A6).** `warp_sum`
   depends only on lane values, never on warp identity. But identity across *different kernels*
   also depends on nvcc's FMA-contraction choice at each inlined call site, e.g.
   `a += d_w[k] * warp_sum(acc)` and `d4 * (d8*sumi − 8*SUM)`. Contraction is a compiler decision,
   not a language guarantee. Write the shared dot and combine helpers with `__fmaf_rn` /
   `__fmul_rn` / `__fadd_rn`, which are never contracted, and the porting rule becomes sufficient
   rather than empirically true.
4. **Make `bpr`/`sbpr` compile-time constants (64, 48, 368, 8).** The lane loops would unroll and
   both load rounds would issue back-to-back instead of load→dp4a→load. Same add order, so
   bit-identical. Probably small on big kernels; may shave the 2.36–3.54 MB kernels' ramp. Cheap.
5. **`SPLIT` drift.** The Makefile passes `SPLIT ?= 128`, while `kernels.cuh` argues for 256 "so the
   cap does not bind until seq=16384". The shipped build uses 128, so the cap binds at 8192. Pick
   one and make the comment true.
6. **`k_attn_split` comment.** It says a warp covers "512 contiguous bytes". The KV layout is
   `[t][kv_head][64]`, so four tokens are four 128 B lines **1 KB apart**. Every sector is still
   fully used, so the 1007 GB/s stands, but it is not contiguous. A head-major per-layer layout
   `[kv][t][64]` makes every slice contiguous. "Transposing was worse" was measured with the
   pre-4z.8 loop and is worth re-testing for V (long context only).
7. **`d` plane caching.** `dot_q4_0_q8_1` reads `qs` with `__ldcs` but `d[blk]` with a default
   load. Use `__ldcs` for the scale plane too (1/9 of weight bytes), especially if you adopt the L2
   streamer in §1.
8. **ARCHITECTURE §12 A6 (why the L2 hint beats `ld.global`).** The most likely explanation is that
   a warp can't retire while it has an outstanding load. The demand loads keep `k_swiglu` alive
   until 2 MB has arrived, which delays `moe_down`'s launch by about the time the prefetch saved.
   `prefetch` has no destination register, so warps exit at once. Check: time `k_swiglu` alone with
   `PF_LOAD=1` vs 0. The kernel should lengthen by roughly a DRAM round trip plus 2 MB of transfer.

## 6. Considered and not recommended

- **Exact greedy argmax from a truncated `lm_head` bit-plane, with an exact rescore of candidates.**
  It is lossless, but a rigorous error bound for dropping the low 2 bits is about 5 standard
  deviations of the logit distribution, so nearly the whole vocabulary survives. Dropping 1 bit
  saves ~33 MB/token (~2%) at real complexity. Low priority.
- **Entropy-coding Q4_0 nibbles or fp16 scales.** At most ~10% of bytes, for a GPU decode cost on
  the order of 10 instructions per symbol at 1.3T symbols/s. That doesn't pay at batch 1.
- Second GPU: out of scope per your instruction. KV quantisation: agree, don't.
